# Answering an Easier Question

Chapter 9: Answering an Easier Question
Summary:  This chapter directly addresses the phenomenon of  substitution  hinted in the previous
chapter – when faced with a hard question, we answer an easier one without realizing it. Kahneman
elaborates with examples. If someone is asked “How much would you donate to save endangered
species X?” they might actually answer “How emotional do I feel about X being endangered?” Similarly,
when polling about political issues, people often respond based on how they feel about the topic or the
person associated with it rather than analyzing the specifics of the question. This is called the affect
heuristic (introduced in Chapter 8) in action – essentially, “How do I feel about it?” stands in for a
complex evaluation. The chapter points out that we often do this substitution unconsciously. System 1
produces a quick intuitive answer that seems relevant, and unless System 2 steps in, that’s what we go
with. Another important aspect discussed is how our current mood or environment can serve as an
easy answer to broad questions. For example, in experiments, asking people about their life satisfaction
on a sunny day versus a rainy day yields different answers – because on rainy days people feel gloomier
and that affects their life satisfaction rating unless their attention is drawn to the weather explicitly.
The present state becomes the easy answer. The overall insight is that many judgment errors or biases
come from this mechanism of substitution. Recognizing when a question is difficult and deliberately
checking if the answer you’re giving is actually to a different (easier) question can improve accuracy.

Key Takeaways (Chapter 9)
Heuristic Substitution (Confirmed): We routinely answer easier questions in place of hard ones
without realizing it. This is a fundamental mental shortcut. The easier question often has to
do with feelings or simple associations.
Detecting Substitution: An easy question usually has to do with something like “How do I feel
about this? What’s my immediate impression?” versus the hard question which might require
analysis or specific knowledge. If the answer came to you almost instantly for a complex
question, that’s a clue you might have substituted.
Mood Influence: Our general happiness or unhappiness at the moment often gets substituted
for more general questions (like “How satisfied are you with your life?”). Unless we consciously
factor out irrelevant influences (like the weather, recent events), those influences will shape our
answers implicitly.
Affect in Reasoning: The affect heuristic is reiterated – our likes/dislikes drive many judgments
that should be factual. “Is nuclear power safe?” often becomes “Do I like nuclear power or not?”
in practice, and the answer follows the attitude, not a technical risk analysis. This underscores
how emotional substitution can lead to polarized views: we may ignore data that doesn’t fit the
simpler emotional answer.
Applications to Studying Technical Subjects (Chapter 9)
Answer the Actual Question: In exams, professors often insert an extra twist to see if students
truly understand or if they’ll fall back on a stock response. Always ask yourself, “What is this really
asking?” When checking your answer, ensure each part aligns with the question. If you realize
you answered a related but different question (common in word problems or multi-step
questions), correct course. For example, a physics problem asks for acceleration at a certain time,
but you find you computed velocity because that was easier or more straightforward – catch such
substitutions during review.
Manage Bias in Study Reviews: When reviewing your performance or deciding what to study
next, don’t substitute the hard question “Where are my knowledge gaps?” with the easier “Which
subject do I dislike?” It’s easy to avoid topics you find dull (because negative affect makes you
think “I’m bad at this or it’s not important”) even if that’s where you need improvement. Use
objective criteria – like past exam scores per topic – to identify gaps, rather than going by what
feels difficult or boring. In other words, separate emotional aversion from actual performance
needs when planning your studies.
Don’t Let Mood Dictate Self-Assessment: Suppose you’re feeling frustrated after struggling
with a tough assignment. You might substitute the question “How well am I doing in this course
overall?” with “How do I feel about this course right now?” leading to an unnecessarily negative
self-assessment. Recognize when your current mood might be hijacking broader judgments.
Perhaps wait until you’ve cooled off and look at actual indicators (grades, progress made) to
assess your standing. Conversely, after a string of successes, you might overestimate your
mastery – so temper that by attempting a challenge or two to ensure you’re not basking in an
affective high that isn’t representative of all material.
Practice Mindful Problem-Solving: When a solution or answer comes to mind extremely
quickly, practice pausing and reflecting: “Is this answering the right question, or just an easier
one?” This habit, though it takes a second or two, can prevent a lot of mistakes. In math, for
instance, if asked for a proof (hard question: logically show X is true), a student might substitute
example (“it works in this case, so maybe it’s always true”). Being aware of the temptation to

Applications to Software Engineering (Chapter 9)
Requirements Clarity: In development, always clarify the real requirements. It’s common to
hear a client request and subconsciously substitute it with a more familiar request. For example,
a client asks “Can you make our system handle more transactions per second?” – a hard question
that might involve architecture rework or algorithms. An easy substitution might be “They want
the system to be faster (latency)” and you might focus on speed instead of throughput. Make
sure you’re not solving the wrong problem because it was easier to interpret. Repeat back what
you think the requirement is in your own words and confirm (this engages System 2 to verify you
didn’t subconsciously swap questions).
Decision-Making Criteria: In project decisions, avoid substituting objective criteria with gut
feeling. For instance, deciding “Which database to use for this project?” could devolve into
“Which DB do I like the most?” or “Which one did I hear good things about recently?” If you
notice a decision conversation is basically trading personal likes/dislikes (affect heuristic) rather
than focusing on requirements (performance needs, cost, scalability), steer it back. Perhaps
make a decision matrix: list criteria the database must meet and score candidates. This forces
answering the hard questions (like specific feature support) rather than the easy one (“Do we
generally feel this tech is good?”).
Code Debugging vs. Symptom Fixing: A specific scenario: when debugging, the tough question
is “What is the root cause of this bug?” A common substitution is “What quick fix will make the
error message go away?” The latter is easier – you might restart a service, or change a
parameter so the immediate symptom stops – but that doesn’t answer why it happened and
could hide deeper issues. Recognize this pattern: if you find yourself just silencing alarms or
patching things without understanding, you might be doing substitution. Take time to really
diagnose, even if it’s harder and slower, especially for critical systems.
User Feedback Interpretation: Users might ask for a feature (hard question: “Does this feature
truly solve their underlying problem?”). A lazy substitution is building exactly what they ask for
without probing (easier: “Implement X as requested”). As an engineer or product manager, think
deeper: what’s the underlying need? Maybe the user says “We need an export to Excel” –
perhaps the real issue is “We need to analyze data outside your app.” Sometimes a better
solution exists (like an in-app report) but if you substitute their request for their need, you might
miss it. So always address the fundamental question (“What is the user’s goal or pain point?”)
rather than blindly the literal question (which might be their own guess at a solution).

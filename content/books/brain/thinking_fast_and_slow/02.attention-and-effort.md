# Attention and Effort

Chapter 2: Attention and Effort
Summary: This chapter delves into the limitations of our mental attention and the effort it takes to
perform tasks that require System 2. Kahneman explains that mental effort is a limited resource – we
can only consciously focus on one demanding task at a time, and when we do, we become effectively
blind to other stimuli. A famous example is the gorilla experiment: people asked to concentrate on
counting basketball passes often fail to notice a person in a gorilla suit walk through, demonstrating
that intense focus on one task causes us to miss obvious other inputs. The chapter also introduces
the idea of the “law of least effort”: both people and our cognitive processes tend to gravitate toward
the least demanding course of action. We naturally avoid heavy mental lifting when we can, which
means if a problem can be solved with minimal thought, we prefer that route. Kahneman points out
that  practiced skills become less effortful  – as you gain expertise or familiarity, tasks that once
required intense concentration can be done more automatically (reducing System 2 load). For
instance, an experienced driver can converse while driving because many driving tasks have become
intuitive. But a novice driver must focus intently on each action, easily overloading their attention. The
chapter’s key message is that attention is a scarce resource: multitasking on two effortful tasks is nearly
impossible without performance suffering. We also learn that switching tasks is itself effortful –
context switching incurs a cost in time and mental energy. Overall, Attention and Effort emphasizes
the importance of managing cognitive load: System 2 tasks feel “hard” because they draw from a shared
pool of effort, and we’re wired to conserve that effort whenever possible.
Key Takeaways (Chapter 2)
Limited Attention: We have a finite capacity for conscious attention. When System 2 is engaged
in a demanding task, we often become “blind” to other information or stimuli (demonstrated by
experiments where focused attention causes obvious things – like a gorilla on screen – to go
unnoticed).
Law of Least Effort: Both cognitively and physically, humans tend to minimize effort. We
naturally prefer to solve problems in ways that use less mental energy and avoid overloading
attention.
Practice Reduces Effort: Repetition and skill development can move tasks from slow, effortful
processing toward faster, automatic processing. As a result, tasks that once demanded intense
focus (e.g. reading, basic arithmetic) become easier and free up mental resources.
No True Multitasking: It’s difficult or impossible for System 2 to fully focus on two effortful
tasks at once. Task-switching carries a cost – shifting attention is itself an effortful process that
can degrade performance, especially under time pressure. To do something well, it’s best to
focus on it exclusively.
Applications to Studying Technical Subjects (Chapter 2)
Avoid Cognitive Overload: When studying complex material (like learning a new programming
concept or solving math problems), eliminate distractions and tackle one thing at a time.
Recognize that trying to multitask (e.g. doing homework while checking email or toggling
between different problem sets) will impair your learning. You’ll absorb more if you devote full
attention to one topic instead of spreading your limited System 2 resources across multiple
tasks.
Chunk and Automate Basics: Leverage the fact that practiced tasks require less effort. Drill
fundamental skills (formula manipulation, coding syntax, physics unit conversions) until they
become second nature. This reduces the cognitive load for advanced problems, allowing your
attention to focus on the novel or hard parts. For example, if you’ve solved many circuits

Use Micro-Breaks and Pomodoro: Since sustained concentration is tiring, study in focused

Step-by-Step Problem Solving: When facing a complex engineering problem, break it into
smaller sub-problems. This not only prevents working memory overload but also takes
advantage of the law of least effort by tackling one small step at a time. Write down
intermediate steps to offload memory. By simplifying each step, you make each mental
operation less strenuous, which helps you maintain accuracy and persistence through a long
solution.
Applications to Software Engineering (Chapter 2)
Focus on One Task (No Multitasking): In software development, it’s common to juggle coding,
emails, meetings, and debugging at once – but Thinking, Fast and Slow reminds us that deep
work demands undivided attention. When debugging a tricky issue or designing a system, try to
minimize interruptions: close unnecessary tabs, put your phone away, and consider using “do
not disturb” mode. You’ll solve problems faster and with fewer errors by avoiding constant task-
switching, since each context switch drains mental energy and time.
Use Automation and Tools: Repetitive or straightforward tasks can be offloaded to tools,
freeing your mental resources for harder logic. For instance, use linters or auto-formatters so
you don’t mentally dwell on syntax, or automate build/test processes. By making routine tasks
effortless, you reserve your attention for creative or complex thinking (where System 2 is truly
needed).
Gradually Onboard New Developers: When mentoring a junior engineer, be mindful of
cognitive load. Early on, everything is novel and requires effort – the codebase, language, tools,
domain knowledge. Don’t throw them into multitasking contexts. Instead, ease them in with
simpler tasks that become building blocks. As they gain experience (and certain tasks become
automatic), they can handle more complexity. This approach mirrors how practice builds
automaticity, reducing required attention per task.
Schedule “Deep Work” Wisely: Identify your peak focus times (morning, late night, etc.) and
reserve those for demanding development tasks. Use low-energy times for simpler tasks (like
code refactoring, responding to emails, or writing documentation) which don’t require as much
intense focus. This aligns your most effortful work with when you have maximum System 2
capacity, preventing overload. It’s also useful to batch similar tasks to avoid frequent context
switches – for example, spend an hour fixing multiple small bugs in one go rather than
intermixing bug-fixing with new feature coding, which would force your brain to constantly
reorient.

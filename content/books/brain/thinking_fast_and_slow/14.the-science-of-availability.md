Kahneman discusses classic studies: people tend to think words that start with “K” are more common
than words with “K” as third letter because we can more readily retrieve examples of words starting with
K (kite, key) than those with K in the third position – even though in English the latter is actually

reports of a plane crash, many might overestimate the risk of flying because those vivid memories are
easily available, whereas car crashes (more common but less sensational) might not be top-of-mind,
leading us to underestimate their frequency. The chapter also outlines factors that increase availability:
salience (dramatic events are easier to recall), recency (recent events are fresh in memory), repetition
(hearing about something often makes it seem common). It shows how availability can lead to
biases: we might think the world is more dangerous than it is if exposed to lots of crime news (because
those examples come easily to mind). Conversely, if something doesn’t make the news (like mundane
good deeds), we underestimate its prevalence. Kahneman emphasizes that availability is a heuristic – a
mental shortcut – and like others, it’s prone to systematic error. He advises that to avoid availability bias,
one should consider not just what comes to mind, but also what might not be readily recalled, and look
for statistics or broader evidence rather than trusting memory ease.
Key Takeaways (Chapter 12)
Availability Heuristic: We judge how frequent or likely something is based on how easily we can
recall examples of it. If it’s easy to think of cases, we assume it’s common; if we struggle to
think of cases, we assume it’s rare.
Biases from Availability: This heuristic can mislead us. Our memory and exposure aren’t perfect
mirrors of reality. Unusual or emotional events stick in memory (making us overestimate them),
while boring or unseen events are underrepresented in memory (leading us to underestimate
them).
Examples: People often overestimate causes of death that are dramatic (like tornadoes, shark
attacks) and underestimate mundane ones (like asthma or falls) because the dramatic ones are
more memorable and publicized. In personal life, you might think you do more
housework than your spouse if examples of your cleaning readily come to mind (your efforts are
salient to you), while forgetting times you didn’t see the spouse cleaning.
Counteracting Availability Bias: It requires an active effort – ask “What factors could be making
these examples come to mind so easily? Are there examples that I’m missing because they don’t
come to mind readily?”. Also, listing reasons for a judgment that go beyond personal
memory (like research or databases) helps. Being aware of this bias is the first step to adjusting
for it.
Applications to Studying Technical Subjects (Chapter 12)
Don’t Rely Only on Memory for Review: When reviewing for an exam, you might feel certain
topics are “definitely going to be on the test” because you recall seeing them often in class or
homework (high availability). But perhaps those stand out because they were tricky or
emphasized, whereas some less flashy but fundamental topics (mentioned only briefly, so low
availability) might be equally or more important. Check the syllabus or past exams to get actual
frequencies. Conversely, if you can’t recall many instances of a concept being used, you might
think it’s unimportant – verify instead of assuming. For example, just because dynamic
programming examples were few (and thus not readily recalled) doesn’t mean it won’t appear;
maybe it’s fundamental and the professor expects you to extrapolate from one example.

because I remember solving some problems easily” – careful, you might be recalling a few
successes and forgetting struggles. Our minds store successes and failures unevenly, possibly

# Norms, Surprises, and Causes

Chapter 6: Norms, Surprises, and Causes
Summary: This chapter explains how System 1 forms an internal model of the world – it continuously
learns norms of what is normal or expected, and it reacts with surprise or causal explanations when
these expectations are violated. Kahneman notes that we are incredibly quick at detecting even
slight deviations from the norm. For example, we notice if someone’s tone of voice suddenly changes or
if an object in a room is missing, often within fractions of a second. System 1 maintains an
understanding of how things usually go (norms) and flags anything unusual. When an event surprises
us, System 1 automatically tries to find a cause or story to explain it – this reflects our deep-rooted bias
to find causal explanations, even for random events. The chapter highlights that surprise is a key
indicator of how we model reality: if you’re surprised, it means your brain had a different expectation
(norm) than what occurred. We are also prone to believe we understand cause-and-effect more than
we do – System 1 might jump to a cause (often a simple, coherent story) for something that might
actually be due to chance. Kahneman gives examples like the “halo effect” (if you like one aspect of a
person, you’ll assume other good things about them), which is a kind of causal storytelling our mind
does automatically (“I like his voice, so he must be competent too”). WYSIATI (“What You See Is All There
Is”) is introduced: System 1 forms a story based only on the information at hand, no matter how limited,
and it feels true if it’s coherent. Essentially, Chapter 6 shows how our intuitive mind builds a world of
expectations and a narrative of causes – useful for quick understanding, but it can mislead us when
reality is more complex or random than our stories.
Key Takeaways (Chapter 6)
Norms and Surprise: System 1 automatically learns patterns of “normality” – what usually
happens in given contexts. It is very sensitive to deviations from these norms; even a slight
anomaly triggers a surprise response (e.g., hearing an odd inflection in someone’s voice grabs
your attention because it’s not normal). Surprise is essentially your brain signaling “this is
unexpected,” revealing what it predicted implicitly.
Causal Thinking: We have an innate tendency to explain surprises by constructing causal
stories. System 1 will immediately try to link events into a cause-and-effect narrative, often even
when events are truly random. We prefer believing “X happened because of Y” rather than
accepting that X might have no meaningful cause we can identify.
Halo Effect: One specific bias from our urge for coherence is the halo effect – our overall
impression of a person (or thing) influences judgments about their specifics. If your System 1
finds someone likable in one way, it will assume other positive traits about them without
evidence (and vice versa for negative impressions). This is a byproduct of constructing a
consistent story (“everything about them is good” or “all bad”).
WYSIATI (What You See Is All There Is): System 1 operates on the information available, no
matter how limited, and ignores what it doesn’t know. It jumps to conclusions using
whatever data it has, aiming for a coherent story. This leads to overconfidence – because if the
story hangs together from the visible information, we feel confident in conclusions, even if key
facts are missing.
Applications to Studying Technical Subjects (Chapter 6)
Check Your Assumptions: When studying or solving problems, be mindful of WYSIATI – don’t
assume the information given in a problem is all there is, especially if something surprises you.
For instance, if a physics question yields a surprising result, avoid immediately concocting a story
like “maybe the laws are different here”; instead, check if you missed any detail in the problem
statement or if an unstated assumption (like frictionless surface, ideal gas, etc.) is implicit.

Use Surprise as a Learning Signal: If a concept or experiment result truly surprises you, that’s a
flag that your mental model (your internal “norm”) didn’t predict it. Dive into that – it means
there’s something new to learn or an old misconception to fix. For example, many students are
surprised by counterintuitive results (like a heavier object not falling faster than a lighter one in
vacuum). Instead of shrugging it off, examine the cause: “Why was I surprised? What norm did I
believe? What’s the actual principle?” This reflection solidifies learning by aligning your
expectation with the correct norm in the future.
Beware of the Halo Effect in Studying: You might have a “halo” bias with subjects or
techniques. For example, if you love mathematics, you might assume you’ll do well in any
quantitative subject and underestimate necessary effort in, say, statistics (or conversely, if you
dislike a professor, you might doubt everything from that class). Recognize this and
compartmentalize feedback. Just because one chapter of the textbook was clear doesn’t mean all
are easy – double-check each piece of material on its own merits. Likewise, critically evaluate
each step of your solution; don’t let the glow of a correct first part make you assume your
subsequent steps are also correct without verification.
Causal Stories vs. Statistics: In subjects like engineering or math, sometimes things happen by
random chance or complex factors, but your intuition may try to force a simple story. For
example, if you did well on an exam after wearing a particular shirt, System 1 might whimsically
link them (“lucky shirt caused it”). Recognize these as superstitions formed by your causal-
hungry brain. Instead, focus on real causality – you did well likely because of preparation. In
study planning, base decisions on data (e.g., “I got problems wrong on topics X and Y; I need to
review them”) rather than anecdotes (“The one time I drank coffee all night, I aced the test, so
sleep must not matter”). Guard against false cause-and-effect conclusions by seeking rational
explanations and evidence.
Applications to Software Engineering (Chapter 6)
Incident Analysis – Don’t Jump to a Story: When something goes wrong in a system (surprise
outage, bug in production), the immediate impulse is to find a cause. While it’s important to
diagnose issues, be careful not to latch onto the first plausible story your brain offers. For
example, if the server crashed after a particular deployment, System 1 might yell “that deploy
broke it!” – a causal story. It might be true, but keep an open mind: maybe an unrelated database
issue happened coincidentally at the same time. Use systematic debugging: gather evidence,
check logs, reproduce scenarios. By consciously slowing down and not letting WYSIATI dictate
(“the deploy is all I see, so that must be it”), you avoid mis-identifying causes. This leads to more
effective and accurate root cause analysis in post-mortems.
Avoid Halo Effect in Evaluations: In code reviews or evaluating teammates, guard against the
halo effect. If a developer is very skilled in one area (say writing efficient algorithms), don’t
automatically assume their design documentation or testing is also flawless. Review each aspect
independently. Conversely, if someone made a silly mistake last sprint, don’t let a negative halo
make you overly critical of all their contributions – they might do excellent work in other areas.
Using checklists or criteria can force a focus on specifics, countering the all-or-nothing story our
minds like to form about individuals or projects.
Expect the Unexpected in Planning: Software projects often fall victim to our poor expectation
setting – we assume a “norm” (like tasks always go as planned). Then we are surprised by delays
or complications, and we scramble for causes (sometimes blaming specific factors while ignoring
the baseline truth that variability is normal). To mitigate this, apply outside-view statistics: know
that it’s normal for software projects to take longer than expected (planning fallacy). Build in
buffers and don’t treat the optimistic scenario as the norm. When surprises happen, analyze

WYSIATI in Requirements: When gathering requirements or user feedback, be aware of what
you don’t see. If a user says “Feature X is bad” with no further detail, your mind might fill in a
causal story (“they must have encountered bug Y, so fix Y and they’ll be happy”). But perhaps
there’s more – other users might have different issues unseen to you. Proactively seek missing
information (ask follow-up questions, consider alternative explanations for their dissatisfaction).
This approach acknowledges that the first story (based on visible data) might not be the whole
story. It leads to more robust understanding and solutions that address root causes rather than
the first assumption.

# The Lazy Controller

Chapter 3: The Lazy Controller
Summary: In Chapter 3, Kahneman explores a core limitation of System 2: it is a “lazy controller.” This
means our conscious, deliberative mind often avoids expending effort even when it should engage.
One role of System 2 is to monitor and correct the impulses of System 1 (acting like an internal
“controller” to prevent mistakes or impulsive actions), but this supervisory control frequently fails
because System 2 tends to conserve energy and not intervene unless absolutely necessary. The
chapter introduces the concept of ego depletion in more detail: exerting self-control or mental effort in
one  task  reduces  the  willingness  or  ability  to  exert  self-control  in  subsequent  tasks.  For

Key Takeaways (Chapter 3)
System 2 Conserves Energy: Our rational System 2 is inherently lazy about expending effort. It
often operates in a minimal mode, doing just enough work to get by, and happily leaves
decisions to the automatic System 1 unless forced to intervene.
Ego Depletion: Exercising self-control or heavy mental effort in one task impairs our ability to do
so in the next task. Willpower and cognitive effort draw from the same pool of mental
energy. After we’ve forced ourselves to concentrate or behave, our “mental muscles” get tired, a
state known as ego depletion.
Effects of Ego Depletion: When people are cognitively busy or depleted, they become more
impulsive and prone to cognitive biases. For example, a depleted person is more likely to choose
a chocolate cake over a healthy snack, or to accept easy answers without scrutiny. They also
might become less patient, use more superficial thinking, or make more stereotypical judgments
when their self-control is worn down.
Restoration: The chapter notes that replenishing glucose (blood sugar) can help counteract
mental depletion – a finding from experiments where giving someone a sugary drink
restored some willpower in the short term. This underlines that physical energy (food, rest) is
tied to mental energy.
Applications to Studying Technical Subjects (Chapter 3)
Study in Focused Blocks: Be aware that your ability to concentrate intensely diminishes over
time. Instead of long marathon study sessions where your self-control wanes and you start
mindlessly rereading without absorbing, break your study schedule into focused blocks with
short recesses. After ~60-90 minutes of challenging study, take a break to recharge (have a snack
or quick walk). This prevents ego depletion from making your study time ineffective – a fresh
mind will catch errors or gaps in understanding that a depleted mind would miss.
Tackle Hard Things First: If possible, schedule your most cognitively demanding study tasks
(learning new difficult material, tackling unsolved problem sets) at the start of your study session
when your mind is freshest. Use your peak System 2 capacity on the toughest tasks. As you get
tired, switch to lighter review or rote tasks that don’t require as much willpower (like reviewing
flashcards or transcribing notes). This way, the lazy controller effect works for you – you save
trivial tasks for when you’re mentally fatigued, and reserve high-focus tasks for when your self-
control tank is full.
Avoid Continuous Willpower Drain: Mix in small rewards or less taxing activities to avoid
constant self-control strain. For example, after solving a hard programming problem, take a few
minutes to watch a short (relevant) video or discuss with a friend – something a bit more relaxed

System 2 strong so you can study efficiently and resist the temptation to procrastinate.
Applications to Software Engineering (Chapter 3)
Watch for Decision Fatigue: After long stretches of coding or back-to-back meetings, your
mental energy for careful decision-making plummets. In such a depleted state, you may notice
you start approving code changes without thorough review or choose quick-and-dirty fixes that
you’d normally avoid. Be mindful of this ego depletion effect – if you’ve had a cognitively intense
morning debugging a complex issue, consider postponing any critical design decisions or
important code reviews to after a break or the next day. A short walk, a meal, or even a catnap
can help you come back with a fresher mind to engage System 2 properly rather than lazily
accepting the first idea that comes to mind.
Use Pre-Commit Hooks (For Lazy Moments): Knowing that your self-control and vigilance wane
when tired, set up safeguards for those moments. For instance, use automated tests or linter
checks that run before code can be committed. When your System 2 is lazy, you might skip
running tests – but if the process forces you (and maybe even blocks a commit on test failure), it
compensates for your momentary laziness and prevents errors from slipping through.
Essentially, engineer processes that don’t rely solely on willpower.
Collaborate for Accountability: Pair programming or code reviews can counteract the lazy
controller. When you code solo and get mentally fatigued, you might gloss over a subtle bug. But
if you are pair programming, your partner might catch it, or just the social aspect will keep your
System 2 more engaged (we tend to not zone out as easily when someone else is involved).
Similarly, knowing that code will be reviewed by peers can motivate you to push past the urge to
cut corners, overriding System 1’s “good enough” impulse with System 2’s thoroughness.
Avoid Overloading Team Members: As a tech lead or manager, be conscious of team members’
cognitive loads. Constant context switching (incidents, feature development, urgent bugs all in
one day) can deplete anyone’s mental energy. If someone has been firefighting a production
outage all afternoon (high System 2 drain), they’re likely not in the best shape to make a careful
architectural decision in the evening. Distribute tasks or allow rotation so that one person isn’t
continuously in a high-depletion mode. A culture that respects downtime (e.g., no expectation to
respond to non-critical messages after hours) will ensure engineers have the chance to recharge
their “controller” and return with full mental capacity for the next challenge.

# The Law of Small Numbers

Chapter 10: The Law of Small Numbers
Summary:  Opening   Part   II   on   heuristics   and   biases,   Chapter   10   highlights   our   intuitive
misunderstanding of statistics with a focus on sample sizes – dubbed the “law of small numbers” (a
tongue-in-cheek   play   on   the   law   of   large   numbers).   Kahneman   shows   that   people   (and   even
researchers) tend to over-generalize from small samples. We have a strong bias to believe that
small samples will be representative of the larger population, when in reality small samples yield much
more variability and extreme outcomes. For instance, the chapter mentions how a few early coin
flips   (say,   5   flips)   could   easily   all   be   heads,   but   many   expect   even   a   short   sequence   to   “look
random” (with some heads and some tails). Similarly, if a disease is rare, a small hospital might have
days  with  zero  cases  and  some  days  with  multiple,  whereas  a  large  hospital  will  have  steadier
percentages – yet people often assume the percentage should be similar regardless of hospital size.

The key lesson:  we are prone to see patterns in randomness, especially with limited data. Our
System 1 wants causal explanations for what may just be luck or statistical noise. The “law of small
numbers” means we erroneously believe “small samples = large samples,” expecting low variability.
Recognizing this bias is crucial in fields that rely on data – one or two data points are often not enough
to draw conclusions, even though our intuition might scream otherwise.
Key Takeaways (Chapter 10)
Small Samples = More Variability: In reality, small sample sizes produce more extreme
outcomes and are less reliable. Yet intuitively, people often expect even small samples to mirror
the properties of the population (we expect randomness to always look evenly mixed, even in
short runs).
Bias to Trust Small Numbers: We often put too much stock in conclusions from limited data.
For example, hearing 3 good reviews of a product might overly convince us it’s excellent,
ignoring that 3 is a tiny sample. Kahneman quips that System 1 “believes in the law of small
numbers” – it expects small samples to be representative.
Causal Stories from Coincidence: Because of this bias, we create causal explanations for
outcomes that might simply be statistical flukes. For instance, if a small town has an
unusually high cancer rate one year, people search for a cause (pollution, etc.), when it could be
just a random cluster given the small population.
Importance of Sample Size: The remedy is understanding that the precision of an estimate
improves with larger samples. Large samples are more precise (less variance) than small ones
. Small samples are prone to “noise.” Therefore, one should be cautious about conclusions
from limited observations – many “miracles” or “outliers” happen by chance in small samples.
Applications to Studying Technical Subjects (Chapter 10)
Don’t Overgeneralize from Few Problems: When studying, you might solve a couple of practice
problems and feel you’ve mastered the topic. Remember the law of small numbers – two or
three problems might not represent the full variety of ways that concept can appear. Avoid the
trap of “I got 3 derivatives problems right quickly, so I’m set for any calculus question.” To really
trust your understanding, practice on a larger set of problems if possible. Similarly, one bad
score doesn’t mean you’ll always do poorly in a subject – small sample variability means you
could have just had an off day or a particularly hard test. Look at trends across multiple
assessments for a fair judgment.
Use Proper Sample Size in Research Projects: If you’re doing any experiments (in science class
or a simulation in engineering), plan for sufficient sample size. For example, if you’re measuring
something like network latency, don’t run just one or two trials and conclude performance is
great. System 1 may be eager to declare success after a small number of good results, but run
many trials and compute averages and variances. This will give you a more reliable measure. Be
prepared for more variability in small sample sizes and thus treat initial results as tentative.
Be Skeptical of Study Anecdotes: As a student, you might hear anecdotes like “I only studied 2
hours the night before and got an A!” Don’t base your study habits on one friend’s story. That’s a
sample size of one, likely not representative. The law of small numbers reminds us that outlier
experiences (like cramming last minute and doing fine) can occur by chance or unique
circumstance – it doesn’t mean it’s a reliable method for everyone. Instead, look for study
strategies that have worked for many people consistently (large sample evidence, e.g., research
on spaced repetition, shows across many students that it’s effective).

Project Data in Stats Classes: If you encounter statistics or research methodology in your
curriculum, pay special attention to how sample size affects confidence. It might feel dry, but it’s
fundamental. Use simulation to internalize it: e.g., simulate rolling dice in a programming
assignment and see how averages stabilize with more rolls. Building this intuition will help
counter System 1’s flawed expectation that “the first few observations tell the whole story.”
Applications to Software Engineering (Chapter 10)
Don’t Trust Microbenchmarks with Few Runs: When evaluating performance, run enough
iterations or use large data sets. A common mistake is to measure something (like a function
runtime) a very small number of times and draw conclusions. Because of random fluctuations
(CPU scheduling, other processes, etc.), a small number of runs can mislead (maybe one run had
an anomalously fast time). Always strive for statistically significant measurements: use many
iterations and compute averages or distributions. Recognize that a couple of tests is a “small
sample” – the results could easily be noise.
Feature Efficacy – Need Enough Data: In A/B testing or feature rollouts, be wary of results from
very few users or short time frames. If only a handful of users tried a new feature and they all
loved it, that’s promising but not conclusive – early adopters might be a biased sample, or it
could be luck. It’s important to gather data from a sufficient user base before declaring a feature
a success or failure. This ties into the idea of statistical significance – ensure your sample size is
calculated to detect the expected effect. Resist the urge to react to tiny samples (like the first
day’s feedback) too strongly.
Hiring and Interviews (Sample of Behavior): An interview is essentially a small sample of a
candidate’s potential work behavior. Realize its limitations. A candidate might do extremely well
or poorly in an hour due to chance (e.g., they got a question on a topic they just reviewed, or
conversely, they misheard one question). Don’t over-extrapolate from that one “sample” alone.
That’s why structured interviews and multiple rounds exist – to increase the sample size of
observations. Similarly, one reference’s glowing review is a small sample of opinion; gather
multiple references if possible. The law of small numbers reminds us not to hang big decisions
on scant data.
Bug Frequency and Randomness: If one part of the system has two bugs in a week, avoid
immediately concluding “this module is badly designed” without considering sample size. It
could be random clumping of bugs (especially if overall bug rates are low). Certainly investigate
quality, but small number clusters can happen by chance. Conversely, if a module hasn’t had
bugs in a long time (small sample of time), that doesn’t guarantee it’s flawless – maybe issues
just haven’t been detected yet. Engineers sometimes refer to “streaks” of failures or uptime;
statistically, streaks can occur without deeper meaning. Always analyze with an eye for whether
the dataset is large enough to justify a pattern.

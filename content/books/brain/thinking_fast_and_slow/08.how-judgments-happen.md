# How Judgments Happen

Chapter 8: How Judgments Happen
Summary: Chapter 8 discusses the processes by which we make judgments, especially in situations of
uncertainty. Kahneman explains that when faced with a complex question, our minds often perform
substitutions and intensity matching. Instead of answering the hard question directly, System 1 finds
a related easier question to answer and uses that as a proxy. For example, if asked “How happy
are you with your life these days?”, one might subconsciously substitute the question to “What is my
mood right now?” (which is easier to assess) and then answer based on that current feeling. This
answering an easier question  mechanism is the essence of many heuristics. Additionally, the chapter
touches on how we try to match intensities across different dimensions: for instance, trying to
judge how “good” a student’s GPA is, one might convert that number into a verbal category like
“excellent” or “decent” and then equate that to an intuitive judgment. Such intensity matching can
sometimes be arbitrary (e.g., how do you match the “size” of a risk to the “intensity” of worry?).
Kahneman also introduces the affect heuristic – our likes and dislikes (emotional feelings) shape our
beliefs about the world. If we have a favorable view on something, we’ll judge its benefits as high
and its risks as low, and vice versa (essentially, our evaluation gets polarized by our affect). The core of
this chapter is showing that our judgments often do not follow logical computations but rather are
driven by these mental shortcuts: substitution (we answer a simpler but related question), intensity
matching (we map one scale to another using our gut), and the influence of affect (emotion). These
shortcuts yield quick answers but can lead to predictable biases or inconsistencies.
Key Takeaways (Chapter 8)
Heuristic Substitution: When confronted with a difficult question, we often unconsciously
substitute it with an easier question and answer that instead. This can happen without us
noticing. For example, instead of estimating a complicated quantity, we might answer how we
feel about it (which is easier).
Intensity Matching: We have a mental ability to compare and match intensities across different
dimensions. We can rate how “good” or “bad” something is on an internal scale (like ranking
from terrible to excellent) and then map that onto another scale. For instance, we might hear a

Affect Heuristic: Our feelings often guide our judgments. If we have a positive affect (emotion)

have a negative feeling, the opposite happens. Our rational analysis of pros and cons can be
skewed by whether we basically like or dislike the thing in question – essentially, “Good or bad?”
feelings drive specific judgments (“It’s good, so all aspects of it must be good”).
Present State Influence: Our current mood or state can heavily influence seemingly objective
judgments (e.g., life satisfaction, as mentioned with mood). This ties to substitution: when asked
a broad question, what’s easily accessible is often our current state or a recent memory, which
then dominates the judgment.
Applications to Studying Technical Subjects (Chapter 8)
Check the Question: In problem-solving, always be clear about what question you are actually
answering. It’s easy to inadvertently answer a simpler question. For example, in an exam you
might be asked, “What is the maximum load this structure can handle given parameters X, Y, Z?”
but you might subconsciously answer “Does this structure seem strong?” based on a rough feel
(substituting a qualitative judgment for a quantitative analysis). To avoid this, re-read the
question and break it down. Ensure your solution addresses every part of the actual question,
not just a related aspect that felt easier.
Guard Against Bias from Likes/Dislikes: If you enjoy a particular subject or method (say you
love  solving things via calculus), be cautious that you’re not seeing every problem through that
lens. You might judge a problem as requiring calculus because you like it, when maybe a simpler
approach exists (affect heuristic at play: “I like calculus, so this problem should be solved with
calculus because that’s a ‘good’ way”). Similarly, if you dislike a topic (statistics, perhaps), you
might underestimate its relevance or assume a stat problem is harder than it is. Combat this by
deliberately considering alternative approaches or perspectives, especially in areas you have
strong feelings about. For instance, ask: “If I didn’t love calculus, how else could I tackle this?”

Awareness of Mood in Judgments: If you’re deciding something like “Should I change my study
plan? Am I doing well enough in this course?”, be mindful of your current mood. A bad day can
make everything seem worse (you’ll undervalue your progress), whereas an exceptionally good
day might make you overconfident. Try to base such judgments on trends and data (grades,
hours studied, topics covered) rather than a gut feeling in the moment. Or at least revisit the
decision at a different time to see if you judge differently. This is especially relevant if a personal
factor (like being tired or upset) might be causing you to substitute “How do I feel right now?” for
“How do I feel about my overall study effectiveness?”

Be Wary of Intensity Matching in Estimation: Engineers often have to estimate effort (time,
story points). Intensity matching can mislead here: you hear a feature description and
immediately your gut says “Sounds huge!” and you map that to, say, “3 months of work” without
detailed analysis. Break down the problem and estimate components. Historical data helps –
maybe a feature that felt small took long and vice versa. Don’t rely solely on the intuitive “size
feeling” because our internal scale might not align with reality. Agile planning techniques
(planning poker, T-shirt sizing) partly help by letting multiple people’s heuristics average out, but
even then encourage people to articulate why a task feels big or small.
The Role of Emotions in Tech Decisions: Recognize when your like/dislike for a technology or
design might be coloring your judgment of its merits. For example, if you personally dislike
JavaScript, you might overemphasize the risks of a front-end project (“it will be unmaintainable,
there will be bugs”) and underemphasize benefits, without objective evidence – that’s the affect
heuristic. Conversely, if you love  a particular cloud service, you might wave off legitimate
concerns because of positive bias. To mitigate this, explicitly list pros and cons and get input
from others who might not share the same emotional attachment. On architecture review
boards, ensure decisions aren’t justified with sentiments (“This approach just feels cleaner”)
without data or reasoning – that could be an affect-driven substitution for real comparison.
Customer Satisfaction Metrics: When gauging user satisfaction, be careful how you interpret
broad survey questions like “Rate your satisfaction 1–10.” Users often substitute such queries
with “What’s my mood regarding this product right now?” Factors like a recent outage or a
delightful new feature will skew scores. To really understand judgments, consider asking more
specific questions or looking at behavior (e.g., usage frequency, retention) alongside self-
reported happiness. In essence, realize that user judgment of your software can be influenced
by recent events or overall brand affection (affect heuristic again). Analyzing trends and context
around feedback can prevent you from drawing the wrong conclusions from a simple score.

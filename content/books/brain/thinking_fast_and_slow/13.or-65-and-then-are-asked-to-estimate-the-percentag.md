Key Takeaways (Chapter 11)
Anchoring Effect: Exposure to any number can influence your estimate of an unrelated quantity
. We tend to anchor on the number and adjust away from it, but not enough. This is true
even when the anchor is obviously random or irrelevant (like the roulette wheel example).
System 1 Priming vs. System 2 Adjustment: Anchoring can occur through priming – the
number activates certain associations (this is an automatic, unconscious influence). It also occurs
through insufficient adjustment – even if we try to reason, we often subconsciously stay too close
to the anchor.
Wide Impact: Anchoring affects decisions in many domains. Real estate pricing, salary
negotiations, budgeting – the initial figures thrown out weigh heavily on final outcomes. People
are typically unaware of this bias in themselves, though they might see it in others.
Combating Anchoring: It’s difficult to avoid, but one strategy is to deliberately generate
arguments against the anchor value (why it’s too high or too low). This can push your mind
to explore a wider range. In negotiations, being aware of anchoring suggests you should be
careful about initial offers (and perhaps make the first offer if you want to set the anchor). Also,
rely on objective data or calculations where possible to counteract arbitrary anchors.
Applications to Studying Technical Subjects (Chapter 11)
Estimating Answers: When solving quantitative problems, be wary of any anchors that might
skew your solution. For example, if a previous part of a question had a certain number, you
might unconsciously let that influence the next part’s answer. Always derive values from scratch
based on principles, rather than intuitively leaning towards numbers you’ve recently seen. If you
find yourself thinking “The answer seems around X,” ask if X came from a proper calculation or if
it might be anchored by a given value in the problem statement (or even the order of answer
choices if it’s multiple-choice). Some instructors deliberately include an irrelevant big number in
a question to see if students anchor on it – don’t fall for that; ground your reasoning in formulas
and known constants.
Setting Study Goals: Anchoring can affect goals too. You might hear that a classmate studied 5
hours for an exam and use that as an anchor, thinking you should do roughly that. But perhaps
you need more time (or maybe less). Better approach: estimate based on your own past
performance and difficulty of material. Another anchor could be a practice test score; e.g.,
scoring 60% on a practice might anchor you to think “I’ll probably get around 60% on the real
one.” Instead, analyze what caused the 60% and how much improvement is feasible with
targeted study – don’t accept the practice score as destiny.
De-anchor Your Self-Assessment: If you performed poorly on early assignments (say, 50% on
the first quiz), that number can anchor your expectation of yourself in the course (“I’m a 50%
student here”). This can become a self-fulfilling low bar. To counter this, gather more data and

proficiency rather than just numerical anchors.
Applications to Software Engineering (Chapter 11)
Estimation in Projects: Anchoring is a well-known pitfall in software estimation. If a project
manager suggests “Would 3 months be enough to build this feature?”, that 3 months can anchor
the engineer’s own estimate. They might counter with 4 months, even if an un-anchored
estimate would be 6. As an engineer, try to ignore proposed numbers and estimate bottom-up:
break the work into parts, estimate each, consider past similar projects. If you’re a lead, avoid
blurting out your guess first – ask the team to estimate independently to avoid anchoring them.
Similarly, when reading others’ estimates, be conscious that once you see a figure, it’s hard to
not be influenced. You might gather estimates anonymously then discuss outliers.
Negotiating Deadlines and Resources: In planning meetings, the first timeline or budget
mentioned often sets the tone. If your manager says “We need this in 2 weeks; can you do it?” – 2
weeks is now an anchor. If you think it’s more like 4, you might second-guess yourself (“maybe I
can cram into 3?”). Best approach is to step back and assess what’s required, then respond with
reasoning: “Given past tasks of similar scope that took 4 weeks, 2 is unrealistic. Here’s why...”
Providing evidence helps break the anchor’s spell. Also consider re-framing – if 2 weeks is
impossible, rather than just saying “not 2, how about 4”, outline what can be done in 2 weeks
(perhaps a minimal version) versus full scope for realistic timeline. This injects new anchors
based on concrete deliverables.
Code Review and Performance Anchors: If you see a piece of code and someone comments
“This is slow,” that qualitative anchor might bias your assessment. Test and measure
independently. Or if an old version of a system had an uptime of 99%, the team might anchor on
that number for the new system’s expectations, even if tech changes imply different realistic
figures. Use fresh data and objective analysis to set targets, not legacy anchors. In performance
tuning, beware of initial results anchoring your belief of what’s achievable (“We got 1000 req/sec
after first tweak, so maybe 1100 tops”). Sometimes after more work you might reach 5000, far
beyond the early anchor. Always keep an open mind that the first number is not the limit.
Salary Discussions: If you’re a team lead making a hiring offer, know that whatever number you
put out first anchors the negotiation. Likewise, for personal salary negotiation, the initial figure
(from you or the company) will sway the outcome. It may be advantageous to be the one to set a
reasonable but favorable anchor. If you’re on the receiving end (like vendor quotes for a tool),
don’t let a high starting price anchor you to think a moderate discount is great – research fair
market values. In all these, consciously adjust by researching: external data points can serve as
counter-anchors or reality checks to ensure you’re not confined by the initial number thrown out.

Information Source Awareness: While researching a project or paper, you might find certain
facts easily (maybe a quick Google search shows many articles on topic A vs. few on topic B).
Don’t assume A is more important just because info on A is more available; it could be that B is
newer or less hyped but still crucial. For deeper understanding, check textbooks or scholarly
sources rather than only what’s readily available on forums, which might give a skewed view.
Availability bias can plague literature research if you equate “more searchable” with “more
significant.”
Applications to Software Engineering (Chapter 12)
Risk Assessment: As engineers, we make judgments about what can go wrong in a system. Be
mindful of availability bias: recent production incidents will loom large in memory, possibly
causing you to overestimate their likelihood and maybe obsessively guard against a repeat,
while neglecting other risks that haven’t happened yet (and thus feel abstract). For instance, if
memory leaks caused the last outage, you might focus heavily on memory profiling (because
that risk is vivid) and overlook, say, security or backup processes that never failed before but
could. A more balanced approach is to use data or checklists (e.g., consider top outages
industry-wide, not just what you’ve experienced, to cover blind spots).
Bug Frequency Perception: If you work on a code module and encountered two tricky bugs in it
this week, you might label that module “bug-ridden” because those bugs are readily available in
your mind. But maybe other modules have more minor bugs that you forgot because they were
resolved quickly. To accurately prioritize refactoring or testing, gather metrics (bug counts over
months, severity levels) instead of just going by what bugs you remember recently. Data might
show those two bugs were anomalies and overall the module is stable, or vice versa.
Hiring Evaluations: After interviewing many candidates, you might recall one candidate vividly
(perhaps they gave a particularly excellent answer or a particularly poor one). Be cautious about
letting that vivid memory overshadow others. Availability could cause you to overrate a flashy
candidate or harshly reject someone who had one notable slip. Use scorecards and notes to
fairly compare. It’s possible the best candidate is the one who quietly did well across all rounds
(not as flashy, hence less memorable). Structured evaluation helps mitigate favoring who comes
to mind easiest.
User Feedback and Product Decisions: Vocal user feedback can create availability bias in
product management. If a few users loudly request Feature X on forums or support tickets, that
feedback is very available and thus seems like “everyone wants X.” But maybe the silent majority
doesn’t care or has other needs. Balance anecdotal feedback with systematic data (surveys,
usage analytics). Similarly, just because you personally remember many complaints about a UI
element (since complaints are salient) doesn’t mean it’s the highest priority problem – perhaps
thousands of users are quietly struggling with another issue that doesn’t generate support
tickets. Always corroborate with broader data if possible.

Personal Experience vs. Metrics: Engineers sometimes judge code quality or performance by
intuition from recent work (“The app feels slow today, must be heavy traffic”) – which might be
colored by immediate availability (maybe you tested during a spike). Instead, check metrics logs
over time. Or if one specific bug has caused you pain recently, you might think “This component
is so unreliable!” but look at uptime logs to see if that’s truly a trend or just a fresh wound in
memory. Fighting availability bias often means stepping back and gathering objective evidence
beyond one’s recollection.
